Where do Australians live — and where is the pressure to find work most intense? This visualisation maps the relationship between population distribution and job-seeker concentration across all 88 statistical regions of Australia, revealing a nation of striking contrasts between booming cities, slower-growing coastal towns, and remote communities.

Part One

Where Australians Live

Australia's population clusters almost entirely along the eastern seaboard and south-western corner of the continent. New South Wales and Victoria alone are home to more than half of all Australians, while the vast interior remains sparsely populated. This coastal concentration shapes everything about how labour markets function across the country.

Population by State, June 2025
Source: ABS Regional Population Growth, 2024–25. New South Wales leads with 8.6 million residents. The Northern Territory is home to fewer than 265,000 people — less than 1% of the national total.
Annual Population Growth by State, 2024–25
Western Australia grew fastest at 2.2%, driven by overseas migration to mining regions. Tasmania recorded the slowest growth at just 0.3%.
Components of Population Change by State
Derived from three ABS columns — natural increase, net internal migration, and net overseas migration — combined into a single stacked bar per state. Queensland and Western Australia are the only states attracting residents from other parts of Australia.

💡 New South Wales loses around 24,000 residents per year to other states, yet still grows — powered by over 90,000 overseas arrivals annually. Queensland gains from both internal migration and overseas arrivals, making it Australia's fastest-growing large state.

Part Two

The Search for Work

Job seeker registrations have grown steadily from 11.9 million in September 2015 to 14.6 million in March 2026 — a rise of 22.5%. The pressure of job-seeking is distributed unevenly across Australia's regions, occupations, and states.

Job Seekers per 1,000 People by SA4 Region
Each region is shaded by the number of job seekers per 1,000 residents. Darker red indicates greater pressure relative to population. The map uses real SA4 boundary data from the Australian Bureau of Statistics (2021 edition). Hover over any region for details. Data: ABS population 2025 combined with job seeker registrations, March 2026.
National Job Seeker Registrations, September 2015 – March 2026
Registrations plateaued briefly before the COVID-19 pandemic period (shaded), then accelerated from 2021 onwards. The pre-COVID plateau serves as a baseline for understanding post-pandemic labour market shifts.
Job Seeker Trend by State, 2015–2025
Five states shown across a shared Y-axis scale, enabling direct visual comparison of scale and growth trajectory. Queensland's steep upward trend reflects both population growth and its expanding labour market.
Top 15 Occupations by Job Seeker Count, March 2026
Sales Assistants (573,153) lead by a wide margin. Registered Nurses (371,889) and Aged & Disabled Carers (364,661) rank second and third — reflecting Australia's growing aged care needs. Software and Applications Programmers also feature prominently despite strong demand for tech skills.
Occupation Mix by State: Share of Job Seekers per Group (%)
Each cell shows the percentage of a state's total job seekers in that occupation group. The ACT stands apart — 32.9% of its job seekers are Professionals, far above any other state, reflecting Canberra's public-sector economy. Western Australia leads in Technicians & Trades (16.3%) driven by its resources industry.
Part Three

The Divide: Where Pressure Outpaces Population

When population and job seeker data are combined, a clear geographic divide emerges. Some regions carry significantly more job-seeking pressure per resident than the national average of 530 per 1,000 people. Critically, this divide has deepened in every single SA4 region between 2019 and 2025 — not one region improved.

Deviation from National Average: Job Seekers per 1,000 People by Region
Each dot represents one SA4 region. The dashed vertical line marks the national average of 530 job seekers per 1,000 people. Regions to the right are above average; regions to the left are below. This deviation is derived by combining ABS population data with DEWR job seeker counts. Hover to identify any region.
Job Seeker Growth by State: 2019 vs 2025
Each line connects a state's annual job seeker total in 2019 with its 2025 figure. Every state moved upward. Western Australia recorded the largest proportional increase at 20.6%, followed by Queensland at 18.6%.
Population vs Job Seekers by SA4 Region
Each point is one SA4 region, coloured by state. Dashed lines mark the median population and median job seeker count, dividing the chart into four quadrants. Regions in the upper-left quadrant — small population but high job seeker numbers — face the greatest pressure per resident. Hover for region and state details.
Change in Job Seekers per 1,000 People by SA4 Region: 2019 → 2025
Each row is one SA4 region, sorted from highest to lowest 2025 rate. The grey dot marks 2019; the red dot marks 2025. Every region shifted right — not one SA4 recorded a lower job seeker rate in 2025 than in 2019. Brisbane Inner City and Perth Inner saw increases of over 120 additional job seekers per 1,000 people in just six years.

💡 Australia's jobs divide is not a tale of remote regions struggling while cities thrive. The data shows that inner-city regions — with the largest populations — also recorded the sharpest increases in job-seeking pressure. Population growth and job-seeking demand are rising together in the same dense places, intensifying competition for work where people are most concentrated.
